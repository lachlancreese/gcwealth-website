What changed on 10 August 2026

From 10 August 2026, a self-managed super fund can't enter a new limited recourse borrowing arrangement (LRBA) to buy residential property. New SMSF borrowing for property is limited to business real property.

  • Existing loans: an LRBA entered into before 10 August 2026 can stay in place.
  • Contracts already signed: a purchase under a binding contract exchanged before 10 August 2026 is also covered by the old rules, even if it settles later.
  • Refinancing: a fund can refinance a loan that existed before 10 August 2026. Aim to replace the existing debt rather than increase it, and check the details with your adviser before changing lenders.

Source: the ATO's guide to the changes to limited recourse borrowing arrangements.

What counts as business real property

Broadly, land and buildings used wholly and exclusively in one or more businesses, at the time the loan is entered into. Examples include:

  • Offices, shops, warehouses and factories leased to business tenants
  • Premises leased to your own business at market rent and on commercial terms
  • Qualifying primary production land

A vacant commercial block, or a building with even a small residential part, may not qualify. Your adviser or accountant should confirm the property qualifies before the fund signs a contract.

How SMSF borrowing works

SMSFs borrow through a limited recourse borrowing arrangement. In short:

  • The property is held in a separate holding trust (often called a bare trust) until the loan is repaid, then transfers to the fund.
  • If the loan isn't repaid, the lender's claim is limited to that property, not the fund's other assets.
  • The loan must be for a single asset. Borrowed money can be used for repairs and maintenance, but not for improvements that change the property's character.
  • Rent and your fund's contributions are used to make the repayments.

What lenders look for

  • A trust deed that allows the fund to borrow
  • The trustee structure, with many lenders preferring a company as trustee
  • Enough money left in the fund after the purchase
  • An investment strategy that supports the purchase
  • Rental income and contributions that cover the repayments, and a lease on commercial terms

Refinancing an existing SMSF loan

If your fund already has a property loan, including one for residential property taken out before 10 August 2026, it may be worth comparing it with what's available now. Rates and features for SMSF loans vary widely between lenders. We count switching costs and any changes to the holding trust before recommending a move.

Costs to expect

Setting up the holding trust and the legal documents adds cost, and SMSF loans often have higher rates and fees than standard loans. Some SMSF applications involve a broker fee, and we'll always tell you about it upfront.

Questions about SMSF loans

Can my SMSF still borrow to buy a residential investment property?

Not with a new loan. Since 10 August 2026, new SMSF borrowing is limited to business real property. A fund can still buy residential property outright with its own money, within the super rules, so talk to your adviser if that's something you're considering.

Can my SMSF buy my business premises?

Often it can, if the property qualifies as business real property and is leased to your business at market rent on commercial terms. Your adviser and accountant should confirm the details for your fund.

My fund already has a loan on a residential property. What happens to it?

Nothing changes because of the new rules. A loan entered into before 10 August 2026 can stay in place, and it can be refinanced.

Can I renovate an SMSF property using the loan?

Borrowed money can pay for repairs and maintenance, but not improvements. Improvements can sometimes be paid from the fund's own cash, within the rules. Check with your adviser first.
